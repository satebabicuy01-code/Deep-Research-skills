# Structural Root Causes of Argentina's Recurring High Inflation and Hyperinflation (1940s–Oct 2026)

Research date: 20 October 2026. Labels: [HIST] = historical, [2023–25] = recent past, [2026] = current data. Items marked "background, unverified this session" in Gaps are well-known figures the writer may want to confirm before using.

## 1. Fiscal dominance: why the BCRA ends up printing (deficits, provinces, pensions, subsidies, monetization)

### Takeaway
The deepest common thread across 1960–2017 is a chronic inability to keep government spending within genuine tax revenue. When markets close, the deficit lands on the central bank, through Treasury advances and profit transfers, or indirectly by rescuing provinces and banks. The money printing is the *last link*; the binding constraint is the political economy of spending (pensions, subsidies, provinces, public employment) running ahead of a narrow, evasion-prone tax base.

### Cited Findings
- Buera and Nicolini (Minneapolis Fed Staff Report 580, rev. Dec 2019) trace Argentina's balance-of-payments crises, three hyperinflation periods, two debt defaults and three banking crises (1960–2017) to "repeated failures to rein in the fiscal deficit". Between 1969 and 1991 the currency lost 13 zeros through successive monetary reforms. They conclude that Argentina's future depends on institutions that ensure "the government does not spend more than its genuine tax revenues over reasonable periods of time." — [Minneapolis Fed SR580 page](https://minneapolisfed.org/research/staff-reports/the-monetary-and-fiscal-history-of-argentina-1960-2017); [EconPapers abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedmsr:580)
- An LSE paper building on that work studies the fiscal roots of inflation from 1970 to 2017. It frames the mechanism as follows: when fiscal deficits are recurrently financed by the central bank, the result is "fiscal dominance". — [LSE Economia article](https://economia.lse.ac.uk/articles/407)
- Fiscal dominance (definition): large deficits and debts "effectively set the terms for central bank action". The central bank feels compelled to finance the deficit by creating money or suppressing interest rates. — [Wikipedia: Fiscal dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_dominance)
- [1980s HIST] The fiscal deficit fell from about 10% of GDP in 1983 to about 4% in 1986 under the Austral Plan, then rose again to about 7% in 1987. — [Wikipedia: Austral plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austral_plan)
- [2023 → 2026] Composition of spending (IMF projections reported by local press): pensions are projected at 46% of primary spending in 2026, against 34% in 2023. Energy and transport subsidies fall from 11% of primary spending in 2023 to 5% in 2026. Over 2025–26 the IMF projects a primary surplus of 1.6% of GDP (2025) and 2.2% (2026, later cut). Interest payments rise from 1.2% to 2.2% of GDP. — [Noticias NQN citing IMF](https://www.noticiasnqn.com.ar/noticias/2025/08/24/319174-fmi-proyecta-que-jubilaciones-y-pensiones-explicaran-casi-la-mitad-del-gasto-publico-en-2026)
- [2023–2025] Fundación Libertad: national public spending fell 5.1 pp of GDP between 2023 and 2025, to 14.5% of GDP. The main cuts were public works (−1.24 pp of GDP) and subsidies (−1 pp). Transfers to provinces and universities fell by about 0.7 pp of GDP. — [Canal 26](https://www.canal26.com/politica/2026/02/10/ahorro-estatal-un-informe-revelo-que-bajo-significativamente-el-gasto-publico-entre-2023-y-2025/); [La Voz de San Justo](https://www.lavozdesanjusto.com.ar/el-gasto-del-estado-se-ubico-en-el-nivel-mas-bajo-de-la-ultima-decada)
- More than half of the 2024 spending saving came from adjusting pensions, which were eroded by inflation and the indexation lag. — [La Voz (Clarín group)](https://grupoclarin-la-voz-prod.cdn.arcpublishing.com/politica/mas-de-la-mitad-del-ahorro-del-gasto-publico-se-logra-por-el-ajuste-en-jubilaciones)
- [2026] The IMF 2026 primary-surplus target was cut from 2.2% to 1.4% of GDP. The first-half 2026 primary surplus of about 0.6% of GDP missed the 0.7% target. The government projects 1.3% for 2026, against the 1.4% target. — [Rio Times: IMF third review](https://www.riotimesonline.com/imf-third-review-argentina-target-cut-2026/); [IMF SLA 2nd review, Apr 2026](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/04/15/pr-26120-argentina-imf-reaches-agreement-on-2nd-rev-under-eff-arrangement)

### Inferences
- The causal chain runs: political demand for spending (pension moratoria, subsidies that freeze utility tariffs, provincial employment, transfers) → deficit → (a) external debt while markets are open, then a sudden stop or default, then (b) BCRA financing via *adelantos transitorios* (Treasury advances, capped by the Carta Orgánica as a share of the monetary base and of revenue, caps that were repeatedly widened) and *transferencia de utilidades* (paper profits from revaluing reserves after a devaluation, paid to the Treasury in fresh pesos) → base growth → inflation and peso flight.
- The 2024–26 adjustment shows the reverse mechanism. Inflation itself was a fiscal tool: real pensions and wages were liquefied through lagged indexation (the Olivera-Tanzi effect works in reverse for spending). Part of the "motosierra" (chainsaw cuts) therefore depends on inflation already having happened. That makes the surplus politically fragile once inflation falls, which is visible in the 2026 target cut from 2.2% to 1.4%.
- Pensions growing to 46% of primary spending shows that the structural problem (the moratoria added millions of beneficiaries without contributions) has been contained, not solved.

## 2. The quasi-fiscal deficit: Leliqs/pases, endogenous money from interest, and its dismantling

### Takeaway
Argentina's central bank has twice (1980s and 2018–23) sterilized fiscal emissions by issuing interest-bearing liabilities. The interest on those liabilities becomes a new source of money creation, a "snowball" that grows faster than the base. By late 2023 the remunerated liabilities (Leliqs plus pases) were about 3 times the monetary base, and their interest cost was estimated at about 8.7% of GDP. This is the same mechanism Rodríguez identified for 1989–90. Milei moved these liabilities to the Treasury in 2024 (pases → LEFI), then dissolved the LEFI in July 2025, with interest-rate turbulence.

### Cited Findings
- [HIST] Rodríguez (CEMA): quasi-fiscal deficits were a major part of the 1989 and 1990 hyperinflations. Central-bank sterilization through debt issuance creates interest payments that can grow unsustainably and feed hyperinflation. — [EconPapers / CEMA DT 649](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cem:doctra:649)
- [HIST] Almansi and Rodríguez (1989): the immediate trigger of the 1989 hyperinflation was the 6 February 1989 abandonment of the exchange-rate parity. The root cause was the deep public-sector imbalance, "especially the central bank's credit operations with financial institutions". — [Search summary of CEMA/RedNIE material](https://rednie.eco.unc.edu.ar/files/DT/396.pdf)
- [HIST] Under Austral, "a big motor of the rising inflation rate was the Central Bank's own 'quasi fiscal' deficit." — [Wikipedia: Austral plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austral_plan); BONEX article: sterilization produced a large quasi-fiscal deficit. — [Wikipedia: BONEX Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BONEX_Plan)
- [2022] At end-2022, BCRA Leliqs equalled 2.5 times the monetary base. — [UCEMA: What can we learn from Argentina's financial system?](https://biblio.ucema.edu.ar/investigacion/internationalbanking-150323)
- [Dec 2023] Chequeado's breakdown of BCRA peso liabilities, about ARS 32 trillion: ARS 7.9 trillion monetary base, ARS 9.5 trillion Leliqs, ARS 14.6 trillion pases pasivos. That puts remunerated liabilities at about ARS 24.1 trillion, or about 3.05 times the base. — [Chequeado: el explicador](https://chequeado.com/el-explicador/de-que-se-trata-el-deficit-cuasifiscal/)
- [2023] Econviews estimate: the quasi-fiscal deficit from interest on remunerated liabilities would reach ARS 16.5 trillion in 2023, about 8.7% of GDP. — [Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/argentina/leliq-y-cepo-cambiario-el-riesgo-que-ve-milei-y-cuantos-dolares-necesita-para-desarmarlos/). Contested by then-BCRA president Pesce, who argued that equating Leliq remuneration with the fiscal deficit is wrong. — [BAE Negocios](https://www.baenegocios.com/economia/pesce-se-sumo-al-debate-de-los-candidatos-por-la-bola-de-leliqs-20231003-0079.html)
- Leliqs were estimated at more than 12% of GDP in Dec 2023 (advocacy source). — [Cobden Centre](https://www.cobdencentre.org/2023/12/can-milei-really-shut-down-argentinas-central-bank)
- [2024] Endogenous emission from interest was still about ARS 9.94 trillion between Dec 2023 and 16 April 2024. — [Bloomberg Línea](https://www.bloomberglinea.com/latinoamerica/argentina/intereses-que-milei-querria-dejar-de-pagar-acumulan-10-billones-desde-el-cambio-de-gobierno/)
- [Jul 2024] The BCRA suspended pases pasivos from 22 July 2024. The Treasury's LEFI (Letras Fiscales de Liquidez; ARS 20 trillion authorized under Res. 40/2024; one-year term; interest capitalized daily at the policy rate) became the main bank liquidity instrument. The BCRA itself stated that remunerated liabilities reflected the need to sterilize pesos "emitted to finance the fiscal deficit accumulated over years". The interest cost moved to the Treasury and must be paid from fiscal surplus. Caputo said "se desactivó una bomba más" ("one more bomb defused"). — [Buenos Aires Herald](https://buenosairesherald.com/economics/argentinas-central-bank-launches-monetary-scheme-with-new-treasury-bills); [Ámbito](https://www.ambito.com/finanzas/luis-caputo-celebro-la-puesta-marcha-las-lefi-y-dijo-que-se-desactivo-una-bomba-mas-n6030723); [El Cronista](https://www.cronista.com/finanzas-mercados/debutan-las-lefi-nueva-referencia-para-el-mercado-que-impacta-en-la-tasa-de-interes/)
- [Jul–Sep 2025] Dismantling of the LEFI was announced 9 June and executed 10 July 2025. Funds that were expected to move into LECAPs went partly to cauciones (repos) instead. Caución rates first collapsed, then spiked above 100% TNA (annual nominal rate). The Treasury held an "emergency auction" six days later and absorbed ARS 4.7 trillion at above-market rates. Reserve requirements (encajes) were then raised by about 8% of deposits. Interbank rates, which had averaged about 30% before the LEFI unwind, jumped. — [Ámbito](https://www.ambito.com/economia/venta-dolares-y-tasa-pesos-la-oportunidad-que-vieron-los-bancos-y-la-clave-la-licitacion-del-martes-n6171614); [UADE report Aug 2025](https://www.uade.edu.ar/media/x3di1zsf/agosto-2025.pdf); [Ámbito, Dal Poggetto/EcoGo](https://www.ambito.com/economia/marina-dal-poggetto-aun-buen-desempeno-electoral-el-programa-requiere-un-reseteo-n6185034/amp)

### Inferences
- Balance-sheet mechanics: when the Treasury draws an advance, BCRA assets (Treasury claims) and the monetary base both rise. To stop the pesos chasing dollars, the BCRA issues Leliqs or pases to banks, which swaps the base for a remunerated liability. Interest at, say, 97–133% TNA in 2023 is paid by creating more base, which must be sterilized again. The stock therefore compounds at the policy rate even with zero new fiscal deficit. With remunerated liabilities about 3× the base, a 100% interest rate implies base creation of about 300% of the base per year if not re-sterilized. That is why the "Leliq bomb" was seen as a latent hyperinflation risk: a run out of Leliqs would turn about 3× the base into spendable pesos.
- Moving the stock from BCRA to Treasury (LEFI) did not shrink the debt. It changed who pays: interest is now paid from the fiscal surplus rather than by printing. This makes the cost explicit and puts it inside the fiscal anchor.

### Gaps
- No BCRA primary series was fetched for the peak ratio of remunerated liabilities to the base (often quoted at about 3–4× in 2023) or for the Dec 2023 "punitorio" negative-real-rate cut of the policy rate from 133% to 100%. These are background, unverified.
- The status of the peso-debt stock and interest burden (Treasury LECAPs/BONCAPs) in 2026 was not verified.

## 3. Loss of money demand, bimonetarism, inflation memory and indexation

### Takeaway
Argentines hold their savings in physical dollars outside the banks: about USD 255–262 bn per INDEC (end-2025/1Q 2026), around 48% of total financial assets. Peso money demand is therefore tiny. A small peso base means any emission produces a large price response (high velocity), and inflation shocks are amplified by indexation and memory. Every expropriation episode (1982 Circular 1050, BONEX 1990, Corralito/pesification 2001–02) reinforced this rational distrust.

### Cited Findings
- [1Q 2026] INDEC (balance of payments / IIP): private non-financial sector holds about USD 262.0 bn in cash and other assets outside the local financial system. — [iProfesional](https://www.iprofesional.com/finanzas/458238-datos-oficiales-revelan-cuantos-dolares-tienen-los-argentinos-fuera-del-sistema)
- [4Q 2025] About USD 254.9 bn, up USD 11.6 bn y/y. This class of assets is 48.5% of all Argentine financial assets. — [Diario Panorama](https://www.diariopanorama.com/noticia/547179/datos-indec-cuantos-dolares-tienen-argentinos-debajo-colchon); [Ámbito](https://www.ambito.com/finanzas/cuantos-dolares-tienen-los-argentinos-bajo-el-colchon-segun-el-indec-n6022269). An alternative measure (net of deposits) gives about USD 218.2 bn at end-2025, down about USD 18.3 bn vs end-2023 partly due to the 2024 blanqueo (tax amnesty). — [Noticias NQN](https://www.noticiasnqn.com.ar/noticias/2026/03/28/330186-cuantos-dolares-guardan-los-argentinos-debajo-del-colchon-y-como-evoluciono-la-cifra-en-los-ultimos-anos). Sep 2025: USD 254 bn. — [BAE Negocios](https://www.baenegocios.com/amp/Los-dolares-fuera-del-sistema-de-los-argentinos-suman-USD254-000-M-20250930-0206/)
- "Only 6 of every 100 savings dollars are in the banks." — [Hoy Día](https://hoydia.com.ar/economia/solo-6-de-cada-100-dolar-ahorro-esta-en-los-bancos)
- [HIST] BONEX Plan (Jan 1990): bank time deposits were forcibly converted into 10-year dollar Treasury bonds (Bonex 89, repayable 1999). About USD 3 bn of deposits were swapped. This was a partial default by the BCRA/Treasury on banks, and by banks on depositors. — [Wikipedia: BONEX Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BONEX_Plan); [Tiempo Argentino](https://www.tiempoar.com.ar/ta_article/plan-bonex-a-30-anos-de-la-expropiacion-de-los-ahorros/amp/)
- [2001–02] The IMF IEO describes a simultaneous partial deposit freeze (corralito), a partial default on public debt (Dec 2001) and the abandonment of convertibility (Jan 2002). — [IMF IEO, The IMF and Argentina 1991–2001](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Independent-Evaluation-Office-Reports/Issues/2016/12/31/The-IMF-and-Argentina-1991-2001-17590)

### Inferences
- The arithmetic of seigniorage is P = M·V/Y. With money demand (M/PY) compressed to a few % of GDP, the same deficit financed by printing requires a much higher inflation rate (Laffer curve of the inflation tax). This explains why a deficit of 3–5% of GDP produced triple-digit inflation in Argentina, while a country with high peso demand could finance it with modest inflation.
- Dollar savings make every devaluation a wealth gain for savers and a cost for peso-income earners. Pass-through from the exchange rate to prices is fast because the dollar is the unit of account for real estate, cars and durables. The parallel rate (blue/CCL) becomes the expectations anchor.
- Indexation (paritarias wage rounds, CER-indexed debt, rental-law indexation, pension formulas) turns past inflation into future inflation. That is the inertial component, and it requires a heterodox "shock" (Austral 1985: price-wage freeze plus a new currency) or a hard anchor (Convertibility 1991) to break. Austral failed because the fiscal and quasi-fiscal root was not fixed (see section 1).

### Gaps
- Money demand ratios (M2/GDP; private peso credit/GDP, about 6–7% around 2023 versus 40%+ in Chile/Brazil) were not verified this session.
- Formal estimates of indexation/inertia (e.g. Heymann and Leijonhufvud, *High Inflation*, 1995) were not fetched. Their thesis, from background: under high inflation, contracts shorten, long-term markets disappear and relative-price variance explodes.

## 4. External constraint ("restricción externa"), defaults, IMF, cepo and multiple exchange rates

### Takeaway
Argentina's growth repeatedly hits a dollar shortage. Imports and debt service need dollars, while exports are concentrated in weather-exposed agro-commodities. The 2023 drought was a major hit. Dollar savers also drain reserves through capital flight. Governments respond with debt (ending in default), IMF programs, or the *cepo* (exchange controls). The cepo creates a parallel-rate gap, which signals future devaluation, suppresses exports and FDI, and deepens the shortage. It is a self-defeating loop.

### Cited Findings
- [HIST] Convertibility collapse: the IEO director framed the core problem as "rising public debt, mostly borrowed externally" that clashed with a hard peg limiting policy options. Shocks from 1999–2000 then produced a slow-moving crisis. Uneven fiscal adjustment, a worse global environment and political instability led to a complete loss of market access and capital flight by 2Q 2001. Critics split between "IMF lent too much without adjustment" and "IMF policies contributed". — [IMF IEO press release](https://www.imf.org/External/NP/ieo/2004/pr/eng/pr0402.htm); [IEO press briefing transcript](https://ieo.imf.org/-/media/ieo/files/evaluations/completed/07-28-2004-report-on-the-evaluation-of-the-role-of-the-imf-in-argentina-1991-2001/trascript-of-press-briefing-argentina.pdf); [IEO Argentina evaluation](https://www.imf.org/external/np/ieo/2003/arg/)
- [HIST, 1989] Before the hyperinflation, the BCRA "had lost nearly all its reserves defending the dollar", combined with a large fiscal and balance-of-payments deficit. — [Tiempo Argentino](https://www.tiempoar.com.ar/ta_article/plan-bonex-a-30-anos-de-la-expropiacion-de-los-ahorros/amp/)

### Inferences
- How the cepo makes it worse: an official rate below the parallel rate (gap often 100%+ in 2020–23) is a tax on exporters, who delay liquidation, and a subsidy to importers, who over-invoice and front-run. Reserves fall further, the gap widens and expectations of a "devaluation jump" rise. That drives faster dollarization and lower peso demand, which brings higher inflation for any given emission.
- External debt in foreign currency links the fiscal problem to the BoP problem. A devaluation raises debt/GDP mechanically, which makes default more likely, which closes markets, which leads to monetization.

## 5. Institutional causes and competing theories

### Takeaway
Argentina's institutions repeatedly fail to bind the fiscal authority. The BCRA charter has been rewritten to allow financing, contracts and deposits have been broken (1982, 1990, 2002), and statistics were manipulated in 2007–15. That destroys the credibility needed for any nominal anchor. Theoretical lenses converge: monetarism (the proximate cause is money growth), the fiscal theory of the price level (Sargent: hyperinflations end only with a credible fiscal regime change), and structuralism/distributive conflict (Olivera-Tanzi, wage-price spirals, external constraint) explain different links of the same chain.

### Cited Findings
- Sargent's "The Ends of Four Big Inflations" argument has been applied to modern cases. Endings require a credible fiscal regime change, not just monetary measures. — [GMU thesis, The Ends of Four Modern Hyperinflations](https://mars.gmu.edu/handle/1920/11933)
- The Tanzi effect: under very high inflation the real value of collected taxes erodes (collection lags), so governments rely more on central-bank financing (Hanke on Venezuela). — [Cato](https://www.cato.org/commentary/friedman-inflation-hanke-hyperinflation.md)
- Policy implication from Buera-Nicolini: only institutions that constrain spending to genuine revenue can stop the cycle. — [Minneapolis Fed SR580](https://minneapolisfed.org/research/staff-reports/the-monetary-and-fiscal-history-of-argentina-1960-2017)

### Inferences
- Distributive conflict: unions (paritarias), firms (mark-up pricing) and the state (subsidies and tariffs, pensions) each try to protect their real share. With full indexation and accommodating money, the incompatible claims are resolved by inflation. Peronist "stop-go" cycles follow the pattern of wage and consumption boom → external-constraint crisis → devaluation → real-wage fall → political reaction.
- Credibility collapse: the 1982 debt nationalization, the 1990 Bonex, 2002 pesification ("asymmetric pesification": dollar deposits at 1.40 while dollar loans converted 1:1) and INDEC manipulation all taught agents that state promises are revocable. That makes nominal anchors expensive (high real rates, short maturities) and dollarization rational.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved on INDEC manipulation 2007–2015. Background, unverified: official CPI about 10%/yr vs private estimates about 25%; the IMF issued Argentina a "declaration of censure" in Feb 2013, the first for a member country; the new IPCNu came in 2014 and normalization in 2016.
- No source was retrieved on BCRA Carta Orgánica reforms (1992 independence law; 2012 reform under Kirchner widening Treasury advances and reserve use) or on the 2010 Redrado dismissal over reserve use. Background, unverified.
- Olivera's (1967) original paper, Prebisch structuralism and Heymann–Leijonhufvud were not fetched.

## 6. The 1989–1990 hyperinflation and the 2001 Convertibility collapse: sequence

### Takeaway
1989–90 was the end-game of fiscal and quasi-fiscal dominance plus reserve exhaustion. Austral (1985) froze prices without closing the deficit. Primavera (1988) collapsed when the parity was abandoned on 6 Feb 1989. Hyperinflation followed under Alfonsín and again under Menem, until BONEX (Jan 1990) confiscated the remunerated deposits and the 1991 Convertibility law imposed a hard money rule. Convertibility then failed because the fiscal problem moved to external debt (provinces plus the transition costs of pension privatization) under a fixed peg with no exit.

### Cited Findings
- The Austral Plan initially cut inflation sharply. Inflation returned as price anchors were relaxed, and the quasi-fiscal deficit was a "big motor". — [Wikipedia: Austral plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austral_plan)
- The Primavera Plan of 1988 was the prelude to the 1989–1990 hyperinflation. — [Rossi, RedNIE DT 396](https://rednie.eco.unc.edu.ar/files/DT/396.pdf)
- Trigger: abandonment of the parity on 6 Feb 1989. Root: the public-sector financial imbalance, especially BCRA credit operations with financial institutions. — [Almansi and Rodríguez via RedNIE/CEMA](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cem:doctra:649)
- BONEX Jan 1990: forced conversion of time deposits into 10-year dollar bonds (about USD 3 bn). It was needed because the government could not pass fiscal reform and sterilization had produced a large quasi-fiscal deficit. — [Wikipedia: BONEX Plan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BONEX_Plan)
- Convertibility was "very successful" at ending hyperinflation. Its failure came from rising public debt, mostly external, combined with the hard peg. — [IMF IEO press release](https://www.imf.org/External/NP/ieo/2004/pr/eng/pr0402.htm)
- The Minneapolis Fed chronology lists the 1985 and 1989 hyperinflations and the 1990 BONEX swap. — [Minneapolis Fed SR580](https://minneapolisfed.org/research/staff-reports/the-monetary-and-fiscal-history-of-argentina-1960-2017)

### Inferences
- Under Convertibility, the BCRA could not print. The fiscal gap was financed with dollar debt instead, so the inflation tax was converted into a default risk. When the real exchange rate became overvalued (Brazil devaluation 1999, strong dollar) and growth stopped, debt dynamics exploded. Exit (devaluation plus pesification) became the "default plus inflation" combination: 2002 CPI about 41%.

### Gaps

## 7. 2018–2023 high-inflation episode and the Milei stabilization (Dec 2023–Oct 2026)

### Takeaway
Annual inflation went from 24.8% (2017) to 47.7% (2018), 53.8% (2019), 94.8% (2022) and 211.4% (2023). It then fell to 117.8% (2024), 31.5% (2025) and 33.4% y/y in Sep 2026, with monthly inflation near 2%. Disinflation came from eliminating BCRA financing of the Treasury, a fiscal surplus, the Leliq cleanup and a nominal FX anchor. It has stalled at around 2% per month in 2026. Reserves are rebuilding, but the risk premium has risen again to about 675 bps on 2027–28 maturity concerns.

### Inferences
- What changed structurally: (1) a primary surplus removed the need for *adelantos transitorios*, the "emisión cero" (zero-emission) fiscal rule; (2) the quasi-fiscal snowball was stopped by moving remunerated liabilities to the Treasury (2024), then the LEFI unwind (2025); (3) the cepo was lifted for individuals (Apr 2025), narrowing the gap; (4) external backstops came from the IMF (USD 20 bn) and the US Treasury (USD 20 bn swap).
- What did not change: money demand remains low (about USD 260 bn still outside banks). Inertia keeps inflation near 2% per month, about 25–30% per year, well above peers. Disinflation stalled as regulated prices caught up. The fiscal surplus relies partly on liquefied pensions and cut transfers to provinces, and was revised down. FX debt service in 2027–28 is heavy with limited market access (spread 600–700 bps). 2027 is a presidential election year, historically when Argentine fiscal discipline breaks.

## 8. Generalizable warning-indicator checklist and cross-country comparison

### Takeaway
The pattern is fiscal dominance plus low money demand plus FX debt and reserve loss plus institutional non-credibility. Argentina, Venezuela, Zimbabwe and Lebanon share direct or "engineered" central-bank financing of the state. Turkey shows that very high inflation can come from forced low rates (monetary dominance by politics) without full fiscal collapse.

### Cited Findings
- In hyperinflations, central banks are required to fund virtually the government's entire fiscal operations. Hanke's definition of hyperinflation is more than 50% per month sustained over 30 days. — [Cato (Hanke)](https://www.cato.org/commentary/friedman-inflation-hanke-hyperinflation.md)
- Venezuela: the Tanzi effect made the government rely ever more on central-bank financing. Hanke estimated annual inflation at about 2,219% (2018-era reading, dated). — [Cato](https://www.cato.org/commentary/friedman-inflation-hanke-hyperinflation.md)
- Lebanon: hyperinflation (about 462% annualized, 2020) after years of central-bank "financial engineering" to fund the government. — [BusinessLIVE](https://www.businesslive.co.za/bd/world/middle-east/2020-07-23-lebanon-the-first-mena-country-to-have-hyperinflation/)
- Zimbabwe: years of using the printing press to finance spending. — [Rabobank](https://www.rabobank.nl/kennis/d011302637-turkse-inflatie-loopt-sterk-op-maar-hyperinflatie-ligt-niet-op-de-loer)
- Turkey: inflation rose sharply but hyperinflation was "not on the horizon" (Rabobank). — [Rabobank](https://www.rabobank.nl/kennis/d011302637-turkse-inflatie-loopt-sterk-op-maar-hyperinflatie-ligt-niet-op-de-loer)
- Yugoslavia's Jan 1994 monthly peak of about 313 million percent is cited as the modern extreme. — [Cato](https://www.cato.org/commentary/friedman-inflation-hanke-hyperinflation.md)

### Inferences (checklist, derived from sections 1–7)
1. Primary or financial deficit financed by the central bank (advances, profit transfers, forced bank lending).
2. Central-bank remunerated liabilities growing faster than the base, with a large interest bill relative to the base (quasi-fiscal deficit).
3. Negative real interest rates on peso deposits, which drive flight to dollars.
4. A parallel exchange-rate gap above about 30–50%, and multiple official rates.
5. Falling or negative net reserves, especially below short-term FX debt.
6. High share of public debt in foreign currency or indexed debt, with short maturities.
7. High savings dollarization and low M2/GDP or private credit/GDP.
8. Generalized backward indexation (wages, pensions, rents, debt).
9. Erosion of institutions: CB charter changes, statistics manipulation, deposit freezes or forced conversions, court overrides of contracts.
10. Commodity concentration in exports combined with a shock (drought, price collapse) or a sudden stop, especially before elections.

Comparison: Venezuela (oil-revenue collapse plus BCV financing of PDVSA/fiscal → hyperinflation), Zimbabwe (land-reform output collapse plus RBZ quasi-fiscal operations → 2008 hyperinflation), Lebanon (BdL Ponzi-like "financial engineering" and a dollarized banking system → deposit freeze and lira collapse, resembling Argentina 2001), Turkey (politically forced rate cuts in 2021–23 with negative real rates, which produced an inflation spike without monetization of a large deficit). Argentina combines all four channels in recurring cycles.

### Gaps
- No current (2024–26) primary data was retrieved for Venezuela, Zimbabwe, Lebanon or Turkey inflation (e.g. Turkey peak about 85% y/y Oct 2022; Zimbabwe official 231 million % July 2008), so these are background, unverified. The Venezuela and Lebanon figures above are from 2019–2020 commentary and are stale.
