**Labels used in the tables**
- **[R]** means reported in the source.
- **[D]** means derived by this researcher: summed, subtracted or divided. Every percentage of revenue is [D].
- The occupancy cost proxy is [D] and equals ROU depreciation + rent (short-term, low-value or variable) + interest on lease liabilities.

## Q0. Year-by-year cost tables (IDR bn and % of net revenue)

### Takeaway
I built full FY2020 to FY2025 opex tables from audited notes for ACES and DEPO, and for CSAP at consolidated level. CSAP does not allocate opex to segments, so Mitra10 cannot be isolated. MDIY has FY2021 to FY2025 plus 9M2026, but its FY2021 to FY2023 figures exclude the MIY stores acquired in June 2024. The headline result:

- MDIY has the highest operating margin, about 21 to 23% in 2024 to 2025.
- ACES has the highest opex ratio, about 37 to 40%, and an operating margin of about 10 to 14%.
- DEPO has a low opex ratio (13 to 18%) but also a thin gross margin, which leaves an operating margin of about 2 to 5%.
- CSAP is a distribution-heavy group with opex of about 13 to 14% and an operating margin of about 3%.

### Inferences
- The three retail formats produce very different cost structures, so "opex as % of revenue" alone is misleading:
  - **DEPO** runs a few very large owned warehouse stores of about 170 to 230 bn sales each.
  - **ACES (AZKO)** runs mall-based, leased, high-service big boxes of about 30 to 34 bn sales each.
  - **MDIY** runs small, leased, low-price neighbourhood stores of about 7 bn sales each.
- Gross margin is set by the merchandise model:
  - MDIY: about 55%, from imported private-label general merchandise.
  - ACES: about 48%.
  - DEPO: about 19 to 20%, from heavy building materials.
  - CSAP: about 16% consolidated and about 25% for retail.
- Opex scales with gross margin.

## Q1. Which retailer has the lowest opex/revenue and highest operating margin, and why?

### Takeaway
On opex/revenue, the lowest is CSAP (13 to 14% consolidated, but mostly distribution), followed by DEPO (13 to 18%). Both have the lowest operating margins (2 to 5%) because their gross margins are thin. The most cost-efficient by profitability is MDIY:
- opex absorbs only about 58 to 65% of gross profit;
- operating margin is about 21 to 23% in 2024 to 2025 (17.8% in 9M2026);
- net margin is about 14 to 16%.

ACES carries the heaviest cost load (37 to 40% of revenue) but still earns a 10 to 14% operating margin thanks to a gross margin of about 48%.

### Cited Findings
- **2025 opex as % of revenue [D]:**
  - CSAP: 14.3% (operating margin 2.7%).
  - DEPO: 18.1% (operating margin 2.3%).

### Inferences
- **MDIY is the most cost-efficient on a profit basis.** Its occupancy and depreciation ratios are high, but its gross margin of about 55% is about 7 points above ACES and its labour ratio is about 5 points below ACES.
  - Several features keep labour per store low: a small-box format, centralised procurement across about 5,000 MR.DIY stores worldwide, and a mostly non-permanent store workforce.
  - Expansion also drives scale: revenue rose about 9x between 2021 and 2025.
- **DEPO has a low opex ratio because its stores are enormous and its land is owned**, so occupancy is only about 1% of sales. Its building-materials mix limits gross margin to about 20%, so the operating margin stays thin.
  - DEPO's low cash-occupancy cost is partly hidden in depreciation of owned buildings and in the opportunity cost of capital. These are not visible in opex.
- **ACES's cost burden is structural:**
  - many staff per store (about 53 employees per store [D], 14,058 employees across 265 AZKO stores plus subsidiaries);
  - mall leases;
  - high maintenance spending;
  - high service levels.
- **CSAP's low consolidated opex ratio is an artefact of its large distribution business**, which has a gross margin of about 12% and low cost. Mitra10's true opex ratio is likely much higher. This is unverified.

### Gaps

- The split of DEPO store ownership (owned vs leased) by store is not disclosed in what I read. Only total owned land is given.

## Q2. How did each cost line trend from 2020 to 2025: COVID, recovery, 2023 to 2025?

### Takeaway
Cost burdens rose for every retailer between 2022 and 2025:
- **DEPO:** opex/sales rose from 13.2% (2020) to 18.1% (2025). This was driven by salaries, advertising, occupancy and depreciation from new stores while per-store sales fell.
- **ACES:** stable at about 37% from 2020 to 2024, then jumped to 40.4% in 2025 (Ace to AZKO rebrand plus expansion).
- **CSAP:** drifted up from 13.3% to 14.3%. Utilities and depreciation rose, while advertising was cut in 2025.
- **MDIY:** rose from 24.8% to 34.2%. This came from occupancy and depreciation after consolidating MIY and opening many stores, but it is offset by a much higher gross margin.

Utilities rose as a share of sales at every company.

- **The Ace to AZKO transition** (licence expiry on 31 December 2024; AZKO launch) was cost-neutral to slightly negative on direct items:

- **DEPO's deterioration is a new-store ramp-up story.** Opex grows with each large store (salaries, security, depreciation), while same-store demand from the property and middle-class segment was weak.
- **MDIY's rising opex ratio is driven by its expansion model** (high new-store depreciation and ROU charges). Its gross-margin step-up in 2024 to 2025 more than offset this until 9M2026.

## Q3. What has management said about cost pressures (wages, electricity, rent) and efficiency programmes?

### Takeaway
All four cite weak purchasing power and the cost of new-store expansion as the main pressures. Specifics:
- **ACES** blames inflation and new outlets for higher costs and reports "efficiency measures for underperforming outlets".
- **CSAP** explicitly cites higher depreciation and electricity/utility costs, plus internal consolidation and efficiency measures, solar panels and slower store roll-out.
- **DEPO** cites a weak property market and middle-class demand, plus a push for "operational efficiency" and omnichannel.
- **MDIY** stresses that minimum-wage increases directly raise store labour cost, and that it practises "cost discipline".

### Cited Findings

**ACES**
- "Cost of goods sold and operating expenses generally increased in line with inflation and higher business activity from the opening of new outlets." Operating profit fell 25.04% to Rp874.70 bn. In 2025 the company opened 27 AZKO outlets and launched NEKA, a mass-market brand with 10 stores. It also cited "store renovations and efficiency measures for underperforming outlets". — [ACES AR 2025, pp.36-37](https://d24vnfmq88m0qc.cloudfront.net/file/CAHI/Compro/1778061457224_17df253e-05c8-4cb9-8876-aec2a98cdf47.pdf)
- The Board of Directors ensured that "the rebranding transition process ran smoothly" while expanding AZKO. — [ACES AR 2025](https://d24vnfmq88m0qc.cloudfront.net/file/CAHI/Compro/1778061457224_17df253e-05c8-4cb9-8876-aec2a98cdf47.pdf)
- Citing Ciptadana Sekuritas, Bareksa reports that ACES's 2025 problem was costs, not revenue. Costs rose "cukup signifikan, terutama dari ekspansi dan pembukaan toko baru", EBIT fell about 39% YoY, and 2026 expansion is planned to be aggressive. — [Bareksa, 2 Apr 2026](https://www.bareksa.com/berita/saham/2026-04-02/laba-aces-di-2025-turun-tajam-ekspansi-jadi-beban-di-tengah-daya-beli-lesu)
  - The roughly 39% EBIT decline is consistent with core gross profit minus opex falling from 1,023.7 to 625.6 bn, about -38.9% [D].
- Kontan (14 May 2025) ran a headline that operating expenses are a challenge for ACES. It reported a 2025 target of about 5% sales growth and about 1% SSSG. The detail is paywalled. — [Kontan Insight](https://insight.kontan.co.id/news/beban-operasional-jadi-tantangan-kinerja-aspirasi-hidup-indonesia-tbk-aces)

**CSAP**
- "Operating expenses were Rp 2.51 trillion, an increase of Rp 47 billion, or 2%... due to an increase in Depreciation of Rp 467 billion, an increase of Rp 64 billion, or 15.9%... and electricity and other utility costs." Operating margin fell to 2.7% from 3.3%. — [CSAP AR 2025](https://csahome.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/AR___SR_2025.pdf)

It decided "to immediately consolidate internally, focus on efficiency, conduct prudent expansion", and opened only one Mitra10 and three Atria stores in 2025. ### Inferences
- Management commentary consistently frames 2025 as a year of **negative operating leverage**: weak demand met fixed costs that kept rising from new stores, depreciation, utilities and wages. The response differs by company:
  - **CSAP** slowed openings and cut advertising.
  - **ACES** cut advertising in 2026 but keeps expanding, with AZKO and NEKA.
  - **DEPO** continues measured openings.
  - **MDIY** keeps opening more than 20 stores a month.
- Minimum-wage exposure is explicitly acknowledged by MDIY and DEPO. Salary per employee rose about 13 to 15% YoY in 2025 at ACES, DEPO and MDIY, and about 10% at CSAP [D]. Headcount fell at ACES, CSAP and DEPO in 2025, which suggests labour optimisation in response.

### Gaps
- No primary statement was found quantifying the 2025 or 2026 UMP or electricity-tariff impact for any of these companies.
- No analyst-call transcripts were accessed. Quartr has ACES earnings summaries, but only search snippets were seen.
- Broker reports from Mirae, BRI Danareksa, Mandiri, CGS and Trimegah were not retrieved, so broker views on cost outlook are missing apart from the Ciptadana view quoted via Bareksa.
